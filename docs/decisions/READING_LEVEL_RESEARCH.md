# Reading-level figures, and the literature they come from

**Recorded 2026-09-25.** Operator direction: the per-level lexicon size,
spread count and words per spread should be researched rather than
estimated. This records what the literature says, what it disagrees about,
and where the project's figures sit against it.

**Read the caveats before quoting any number here.** The vocabulary
estimates in particular are contested by more than an order of magnitude,
and this file exists partly to say so.

## Vocabulary size by age

**The best-supported developmental series is Biemiller and Slonim (2001)**,
who count *root words* rather than word forms.

| Point | Root words known, average |
| --- | --- |
| End of grade 2 | **about 6,000** |
| End of grade 6 | **about 10,000** |

The spread within a grade is larger than the growth between grades: at the
end of grade 2 the lowest quartile averages about 4,000 and the highest
about 8,000, **a gap of 3,000 root words inside one year group**.

Nagy and Anderson (1984) put the number of distinct word families a
student meets between kindergarten and grade 12 at about **88,700**, of
which roughly half are learned, which is 3,000 to 4,000 a year.

### Why "about 10,000 at level two" is defensible and needs restating

Level two is entering fourth grade, which is the end of grade 3.
Interpolating Biemiller between 6,000 at end of grade 2 and 10,000 at end
of grade 6 gives roughly **7,000 root words**.

**The operator's figure of 10,000 is a count of words, not of root words**,
and the two differ by every inflection and derivative. This project's
lexicon counts forms separately, so 10,000 forms against roughly 7,000
root words is the same claim in different units. The figures agree.

### The disagreement is real and large

Estimates of adult vocabulary range from **40,000** words for college
seniors (Nagy and Herman) to **200,000** for undergraduates (Hartmann), and
a lemma-based count gives **16,785** for a college student (Brysbaert and
colleagues). That is a twelvefold spread on the same population.

The disagreement is methodological rather than empirical. It turns on what
counts as a word, how words are sampled from a dictionary, and how
knowledge is tested, and dictionary-sampling estimates depend directly on
the size of the dictionary sampled.

**So no figure here should be treated as measured.** They are the most
defensible available and they are estimates.

## Book length by age

Trade publishing figures, which are conventions rather than research.

| Category | Age | Words | Pages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Picture book | 3 to 5 | 200 to 1,000 | **32** |
| Early reader | 5 to 8 | 1,500 to 5,000 | 32 to 64 |
| Chapter book | 6 to 9 | 10,000 to 30,000 | 40 to 150 |
| Lower middle grade | 8 to 10 | 10,000 to 25,000 | — |
| Upper middle grade | 10 to 12 | 25,000 to 50,000 | — |

The thirty-two page picture book is the strongest of these, and it is a
binding fact rather than a stylistic one, which is why this project's
signature rule was already right.

## Where the project's figures sit

| Level | Reader | Project figure | Against the literature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | entering kindergarten | 16 spreads, 320 to 1,280 words | **Inside** the 200 to 1,000 picture-book range at the low end, over it at the high end |
| 2 | entering grade 4 | 64 spreads, 12,800 to 19,200 words | **Inside** chapter book and lower middle grade |
| 3 | entering grade 8 | 112 spreads, 44,800 to 67,200 words | **At or above** upper middle grade, into young adult |
| 4 | entering grade 12 | 160 spreads, 64,000 to 96,000 words | **Young adult to adult**, which is right for the age |

**Three of the four land inside a published range without having been set
from one**, which is weak corroboration and worth saying is weak: the
ranges are wide and a figure can sit inside one by accident.

**Level one's upper bound of 1,280 words exceeds the picture-book
convention of 1,000.** A book at the top of the project's band is longer
than the trade standard. Either the tolerance of thirty words a spread is
wide, or these are early readers rather than picture books. Not resolved
here.

## Lexicon targets per level

| Level | Reader | Target | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | entering kindergarten | 845, measured | What the corpus needed to close |
| 2 | entering grade 4 | **about 10,000** | Operator figure, consistent with Biemiller at about 7,000 root words |
| 3 | entering grade 8 | **about 25,000** | Interpolating Biemiller's growth past grade 6 |
| 4 | entering grade 12 | **about 40,000** | Nagy and Herman's lower adult figure |
| 5 and up | university and beyond | **open** | The corpus terminates in real literature, which consults no word list |

**Levels three and four are the weakest rows in this file.** Biemiller does
not extend past grade 6 and the adult figures are the contested ones, so
these are extrapolations between two numbers that disagree by a factor of
twelve at the far end. They are placed so that later work has something to
argue with rather than nothing.
