# What three character standards agree and disagree about

**Recorded 2026-09-24.** A comparison of the first codified level of
character material in Japan, Taiwan, and mainland China, run because one of
them supplied eighteen concepts to the graph and the other two were not
checked before that happened.

Epistemic status is marked throughout. **Verified** was read from a source.
**Derived** is arithmetic on published figures. **Inferred** is my reading
and is not established.

## The numbers

| | Japan | Taiwan | Mainland China |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Codified unit | grade | publisher's grade | two-grade band |
| The standard fixes | **which characters**, by name | **neither** | **how many**, not which |
| First level, read | **80** | **670** cumulative | **1600** across grades 1 and 2 |
| First level, write | previous grade's list | **547** | **800** |
| First volume | not applicable | ~94 *derived* | ~300 *derived* |

Verified. Japan assigns 80 characters to grade one within a 1026-character
table across six grades. Taiwan's ministry specifies competencies and
leaves selection to publishers, and one publisher reaches 670 in grade one
with 547 for writing. Mainland China specifies 1600 recognised and 800
written across grades one and two, without naming characters.

Derived. A published comparison of mainland and Taiwan first-grade first
volumes reports 61 characters in common, 20.33 percent of one and 64.89
percent of the other. Those percentages imply volumes of roughly 300 and
94.

## Every one of them separates recognising from producing. Three ways

**This is the finding the comparison was worth running for.**

**Japan separates by lag.** One list, and each grade reads the characters
assigned to it while writing the characters assigned to the previous grade.
Production trails recognition by a year over the same material.

**Mainland China separates by subset.** Two tables, 1600 and 800, at a
two-to-one ratio. Recognise twice what you can produce.

**Taiwan separates by near-parity.** One integrated table, 670 against 547,
about 1.22 to one.

No region asks a beginner to produce everything it asks them to recognise.

## Japan's eighty is a writing-system fact, not a pedagogical one

*Inferred, and it matters for how the kanji exercise should be read.*

Japanese writes grammar and any unknown content word in kana, permanently
and acceptably, so a first-grader reads fluently with 80 characters plus a
syllabary. Chinese has no such fallback. Zhuyin and pinyin are scaffolds
that are taught and then withdrawn, so the character load must be
front-loaded far harder.

**So the 80 should not be read as a claim that a six-year-old holds only
eighty concepts.** Taiwan at 670 and mainland at roughly 800 a year are
describing the same child. The concept count a first-grader carries is much
larger than the smallest list suggests, and the smallest list is small for
reasons that have nothing to do with the child.

## What follows for this project

**The level-one concept count is probably low.** Forty-eight concepts are
scheduled at level one. The two standards that cannot lean on a syllabary
put a first-grader nearer 600 to 800 characters. Characters are not
concepts and the ratio is not one to one, but the direction is consistent
and the current figure sits below all three comparators rather than between
them. *Inferred.*

**This supports the lexicon expansion already made**, from 64 level-one
content words to 508, rather than arguing it went too far.

**Our vocabulary ceiling has no recognition tier, which is stricter than
any of the three.** A record at level L may use no word licensed above L.
That is a production ceiling applied to every word, where all three human
curricula admit a wider recognition set than production set.

Whether that transfers is a real question and not a settled one. Under
next-token prediction every token in the corpus is both read and produced,
so the human split does not carry over mechanically. The nearest analogue
is the distinction the schedule already draws for concepts, between a unit
that **teaches** a concept and a record that merely **uses** one, which the
vocabulary does not draw for words.

**Recorded as an option, not a recommendation.** A second tier would let a
record use a word slightly above its level in a scaffolded context, which
is how vocabulary is actually acquired, at the cost of training the model
to produce above-level words. The argument runs both ways and nothing here
settles it. It is plausibly implicated in the 41 drafts still rejected at
the ceiling in the last measured run, and that has not been tested.

**Our schedule is the most prescriptive artifact of the four.** Japan fixes
which characters. Mainland fixes how many. Taiwan fixes neither. We fix
which concepts, which words, and at which level. That is justified, because
the ordering ablation cannot test an ordering nobody wrote down, but it is
worth knowing that no national curriculum constrains itself this tightly.

## What this does not establish

The 61-character overlap between mainland and Taiwan is about **character
selection, not concept selection**, and concept-level agreement has not
been measured. The correction in `KANJI_CROSS_REFERENCE.md` carries that
point and is not repeated here.

Taiwan's figures come from one publisher rather than from the ministry,
because the ministry publishes no count. They should not be read as a
national standard in the sense the other two are.

No divergence analysis was run. Which concepts appear in one region's first
level and not another's is the question with the most signal in it, and it
remains unanswered.
